Upscaler Resolution Calculator: what each mode really renders

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 18, 20264 min readHow we check pages

“Quality”, “Balanced” and “Performance” sound the same in every game, but they are not the same number of pixels. Pick your output resolution and this calculator shows the internal render resolution of every documented DLSS, FSR and XeSS preset, how many megapixels that is and what share of your display’s pixels the game actually shades.

How the calculator works

An upscaler preset is a division. Each vendor documents a scale factor per axis for every preset, and the game divides the output width and height by it. DLSS Quality has the factor 1.5, so a 2560×1440 output is rendered at 2560 ÷ 1.5 by 1440 ÷ 1.5, which is 1707×960. Because both axes shrink, the pixel count falls with the square of the factor: 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25, so Quality shades 1 ÷ 2.25 = 44.4% of the output pixels.

The calculator does exactly this for every preset and rounds to whole pixels. The megapixel column is width × height ÷ 1,000,000; the bar shows the share of the output pixels. The factors come from three developer documents: NVIDIA’s DLSS Programming Guide, AMD’s FSR SDK documentation and Intel’s XeSS-SR Developer Guide. They are listed under Sources, and none of the numbers on this page is a measurement of ours.

Same name, different pixels

The preset names look standardized, but only some of them are. DLSS and FSR agree on Quality (1.5×), Performance (2.0×) and Ultra Performance (3.0×), and differ slightly on Balanced: NVIDIA documents 1.724×, AMD 1.7×. Intel’s names sit one step lower since XeSS 1.3: XeSS Quality uses 1.7×, roughly what the others call Balanced, and XeSS Balanced uses 2.0×, exactly what the others call Performance.

Two practical consequences follow. If you switch a game from DLSS or FSR to XeSS and keep “Quality”, the game renders about 22% fewer pixels than before (34.6% instead of 44.4% of the output). And a game that updated its XeSS version from before 1.3 to 1.3 or newer renders fewer pixels under the same preset name, unless the developer kept the legacy factors – a change worth checking after a patch that mentions XeSS. How to read the result

Internal resolution tells you how much real detail the upscaler has to work with. The same preset gives very different inputs on different displays: Performance mode renders 1920×1080 for a 4K display but only 960×540 for a 1080p display. The option “Mark modes that render fewer lines than…” highlights every preset that falls under a line count you choose, so you can see at a glance which presets get thin on your screen. Which floor looks acceptable is your call – compare two presets in one scene and trust your eyes.

Share of output pixels is the number that relates to GPU load. Most per-pixel work scales with it, so going from Quality (44.4%) to Performance (25%) removes almost half of the shaded pixels. It is not a frame-rate prediction: the upscaler itself costs time, parts of a frame do not depend on resolution, and if the CPU is the limit the frame rate barely moves. To find out which case applies to you, use the Bottleneck Finder, and verify a change with the Settings Test Log.

Render-scale slider covers games that offer a percentage instead of presets, and engine upscalers such as TSR. The percentage is per axis as well: 67% per axis is the same as the 1.5× Quality factor.

What the calculator does not know

The factors are the documented presets, not a promise about a particular game. Developers may choose their own ratios, offer a free slider, or combine an upscaler with dynamic resolution, where the internal resolution changes from frame to frame. Rounding can differ by a pixel between implementations. If a game shows its internal resolution in the menu, that number wins. Console games rarely expose presets at all; there the developer picks the internal resolution per mode, see PS5 performance and fidelity modes.

Frequently asked questions

What resolution does DLSS Quality render at 1440p?

NVIDIA documents a factor of 1.5 per axis for Quality, so a 2560×1440 output is rendered at 1707×960, which is 44.4% of the output pixels.

Is XeSS Quality the same as DLSS Quality?

No. Since XeSS 1.3, Intel documents 1.7× for Quality, while DLSS and FSR Quality use 1.5×. At 4K that is 2259×1271 for XeSS against 2560×1440 for DLSS and FSR.

Why does Performance mode look worse at 1080p than at 4K?

The factor is the same, 2.0× per axis, but the input is not: 4K output is rendered at 1920×1080, a 1080p output at only 960×540. The upscaler has a quarter of the real pixels to work with.

Does a lower internal resolution always raise the frame rate?

Only when the graphics card is the limit. The pixel share describes how much less the GPU has to shade; if the CPU limits the frame rate, fewer pixels change little.
