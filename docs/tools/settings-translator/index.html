Settings Translator: what each option costs, and what to lower first

By the Patch Play Guide editorial team, AdStern

Reviewed November 2, 20265 min readHow we check pages

Graphics menus use different names for the same ideas, and rarely say what an option costs. The Settings Translator lists twenty common options with the typical load they put on the graphics card (GPU), the processor (CPU) and video memory (VRAM). Choose what limits your system and the options that usually help most with the least visible loss move to the top.

How to read the matrix

Every card shows one option and three meters: GPU, CPU and VRAM. Each meter has four steps – minimal, low, moderate, high – and describes the typical extra load of the higher setting compared with a lower one in current PC games. “Visible loss” estimates how noticeable a one- or two-step reduction usually is: subtle, noticeable or obvious.

What the load levels mean
LevelMeaningExample
minimalUsually no measurable relief when lowered on current hardwareAnisotropic filtering on GPU time
lowSmall relief; worth it only when you need the last few framesPost-processing effects
moderateClear relief in many games, especially at the top presetShadow quality, ambient occlusion
highOne of the biggest levers for this resourceRender resolution on the GPU, crowd density on the CPU, textures on VRAM

These are deliberately qualitative, conservative ratings. They summarize long-standing, widely documented behavior – for example that render resolution is the strongest GPU lever and that texture quality mostly fills video memory – rather than measurements from one game on one PC. Real costs differ between engines, and one game’s “High” shadows can be another game’s “Ultra”. Use the matrix to decide what to test first, then confirm the effect in your own game with a short before-and-after test.

How the lower-first list is chosen

When you pick a bottleneck, every option gets a simple score: three times its load on that resource, minus its visible loss. Options that put a moderate or high load on the bottleneck and do not have an obvious visual cost are marked Lower first, up to five of them. Frame generation is excluded on purpose – it is not something you lower to gain performance, and it has its own guide.

Lower-first lists generated from the matrix
BottleneckLower first, in this orderLeave alone (little relief for this bottleneck)
GPUVolumetric fog, clouds and light → Upscaler mode (DLSS / FSR / XeSS / TSR) → Ray-traced lighting / global illumination → Ray-traced reflections → Shadow qualityTexture filtering (anisotropic)
CPUCrowd, traffic and NPC density → View distance / level of detail → Foliage and grass density → Physics and destruction detailRender resolution / render scale, Upscaler mode (DLSS / FSR / XeSS / TSR), Texture quality, Texture filtering (anisotropic)
VRAMTexture quality → Ray-traced lighting / global illumination → Ray-traced reflectionsTexture filtering (anisotropic), Screen-space reflections, Post-processing, Particle and effects quality

This table is built from the same data as the tool above, so the two can never disagree.

The lists match common advice for a reason. On a GPU-limited system, fewer pixels (an upscaler mode) and cheaper lighting and fog buy the most frames. On a CPU-limited system, resolution changes do almost nothing; what helps is fewer objects and characters to prepare each frame – view distance, crowds, physics. When VRAM runs full, texture quality is the first lever, followed by memory-hungry ray tracing.

“Not sure” sorts by combined load and shows no lower-first list. If you do not know what limits you, the Bottleneck Finder or the 5-minute bottleneck check will tell you; the finder also preselects the right bottleneck here when it reaches a result.

Why the same change helps one PC and not another

A frame is only as fast as its slowest stage. If the GPU needs 20 milliseconds per frame and the CPU 12, you are GPU-bound at about 50 frames per second; making the CPU’s work lighter changes nothing until the GPU gets faster. Lower the render resolution until the GPU needs 10 milliseconds and the CPU becomes the limit at about 83 frames per second – from then on, only CPU-side options help. This is why “best settings” lists that ignore your hardware are often wrong for you.

Why there are no FPS numbers here

Many sites publish tables like “shadows: −12% FPS”. Such numbers are only true for the game, scene, hardware, driver and resolution they were measured on, and they are often copied far beyond that. We publish no per-game measurements on this page because a percentage without its test conditions misleads more than it helps. The qualitative levels tell you what to test; your own before-and-after test tells you the number for your system, and our testing guide shows how to make that number reliable.

Consoles, Steam Deck and handheld PCs

On PS5, Xbox and Switch 2 you usually choose a mode instead of single options. A performance mode lowers the render resolution and some effects to hold a higher frame rate; a quality or fidelity mode does the opposite. The matrix still explains the trade-off behind each mode – see PS5 performance vs fidelity modes.

Handheld PCs such as the Steam Deck share one small power budget between CPU and GPU. Render resolution, an upscaler mode and a frame cap matter more there than any single effect, as explained in Steam Deck: frame cap, TDP and battery basics. If you save your platform under My setup, the translator shows a matching note above the matrix. Your choice stays in your browser.

Frequently asked questions

Are the load levels measured?

No. They are qualitative ratings of typical behavior in current PC games, chosen conservatively. They tell you which options are worth testing first, not how many frames you will gain.

Why is texture quality not on the GPU lower-first list?

As long as textures fit into video memory, higher texture quality costs little GPU time. It becomes the first lever only when VRAM is full.

Why is frame generation marked as a special case?

Frame generation increases the displayed frame rate, but input still follows the rendered frames and latency rises slightly. It is not an option you lower to relieve a bottleneck.

What if my game uses different names?

The “also called” line under each option lists common alternatives, and the search field matches them. If an option is missing, look for the closest description of what it does.

Is my bottleneck choice stored?

Only in your browser’s local storage, so the page remembers it next time. Nothing is sent to us.
