VRR and 120 Hz on consoles: setup guide

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 24, 20269 min readHow we check pages

A 120 Hz signal and variable refresh rate (VRR) can make console games feel smoother – but only if the TV, the HDMI port, the cable, the console and the game all play along. One wrong link and the option is greyed out or silently off. This guide goes through the chain in order, with the settings in the words of Sony, Microsoft and Nintendo, and explains what VRR can and cannot fix.

What 120 Hz and VRR do

120 Hz output lets the display refresh twice as often as a 60 Hz signal. Games can then run at up to 120 fps, or use 40 fps modes that fit evenly into 120 Hz (three refreshes per frame).

Variable refresh rate lets the display wait for each new frame instead of refreshing on a fixed clock. Without it, a game running at, say, 52 fps on a 60 Hz display either tears (frames switch mid-refresh) or judders (some frames are shown twice). With VRR, the display refreshes at 52 Hz. Sony describes the effect as minimizing frame pacing issues and screen tearing; Microsoft as reducing stuttering and screen tears. VRR does not make the game faster; it makes uneven frame rates look even.

The TV setting that unlocks HDMI 2.1 has a different name on every brand; the TV’s manual names it. Without it, the console may only see a 60 Hz display.

Console settings

PS5 – Settings → Screen and Video → Video Output. Sony’s support page: 120 Hz Output – “Turn on to enable 120 Hz video output for your TV and games that support 120 Hz frame rate”; turn it off if those games show output problems on a monitor. VRR – “Allow the refresh rate to adjust in real time while using TVs and games that support VRR”, with results depending on the TV; the sub-option Apply to Unsupported Games stabilizes the frame rate of games without VRR support and should be turned off if a game behaves unexpectedly. Sony adds that with VRR on, a TV with ALLM switches to low-latency mode even if ALLM is set to off, and that VRR is disabled during 8K output on PS5 Pro. If the TV supports 1440p, Test 1440p Output checks 60 Hz, 120 Hz and VRR at that resolution.

Xbox Series X|S – Profile & system → Settings → General → TV & display options. Set Refresh rate to 120 Hz; Xbox Support: 4K at 120 Hz needs a Series X, an Ultra High Speed HDMI cable that supports HDMI 2.1 and an HDMI 2.1 TV, while a Series S or a TV without HDMI 2.1 uses 120 Hz at a lower resolution. Under Video modes, tick Allow variable refresh rate (only offered with an HDMI-VRR or FreeSync TV) and Allow auto low-latency mode. 4K TV details shows what the console detected.

Switch 2 – System Settings → Display: TV resolution, 120Hz output and, since system update 23.0.0 (September 2026), the VRR settings. VRR in TV mode needs a dock firmware update (System Settings → System → Dock Update), a TV that supports VRR and a game that supports it; Nintendo: the video is then output “at a refresh rate that matches the game’s processing”. 120 fps on a TV is possible at 1080p and 1440p, not at 4K. Details in Switch 2 docked vs handheld.

VRR windows and what VRR cannot fix

Every VRR display has a window – a lowest and a highest refresh rate it can follow; the highest is the panel’s maximum. Inside the window, VRR works as described. Below it, the display falls back to fixed refresh, unless the system uses low framerate compensation: each frame is shown two or more times so that the refresh rate lands inside the window again. AMD’s example: a 60–144 Hz display can sync a 40 fps game by doubling each frame to 80 Hz.

The lower limit differs from display to display; it is in the manual or in independent measurements. That has two consequences. A steady 30 fps mode gains little if the window starts above 30 Hz and no doubling happens. And a performance mode that dips from 60 into the high 40s stays smooth as long as those dips stay inside the window.

VRR also cannot fix stutter: a single frame that takes 100 ms is still visible, VRR or not. For the difference between low frame rates and stutter, see FPS, frame time and 1% lows.

Worked example: a frame rate against a VRR window

Whether VRR can follow a frame rate is arithmetic. Inside the window the display refreshes when a frame arrives. Below the window there is one way out: if a whole multiple of the frame rate lies inside the window, each frame can be shown two or three times. AMD’s current FreeSync tier table marks low framerate compensation for all three FreeSync tiers, and its FAQ calls it mandatory for FreeSync Premium and Premium Pro. The table runs the numbers for two example windows – look up the real window of your display in its manual.

The windows are examples, not the specification of any console or TV. “Each frame 2×” only happens if the display or the console performs low framerate compensation.

The pattern is worth remembering. A window whose top is at least twice its bottom (48–120 Hz) has no gaps: every frame rate below the window has a multiple inside it. A narrow window (40–60 Hz) has gaps – 24 fps fits only doubled to 48 Hz, and anything between 30 and 40 fps has no multiple that fits, so a game that dips to 35 fps falls out of VRR there. This is the reason a 120 Hz panel matters for VRR even if a game never reaches 120 fps.

AMD’s developer documentation describes the same window in time instead of rate: for a 64–120 Hz window, the time between two refreshes has to stay between 8.33 ms and 15.625 ms, and outside it tearing is likely.

Troubleshooting, in the makers’ words

  • No picture, or 1080p instead of 4K 120 Hz (PS5). Sony’s checklist: does the TV support 4K 120 Hz, does it run the latest firmware, is the cable the one from the box or an Ultra High Speed cable, is the console connected directly or through a receiver, is it on the TV’s HDMI 2.1 port, and are Resolution (Automatic or 2160p) and Video Transfer Rate (Automatic) set as expected.
  • Flickering at 4K (PS5). Sony suggests setting Video Transfer Rate to −1 or −2, which limits 4K 120 Hz output.
  • Display issues with a receiver (Xbox). Xbox Support: turn VRR off or connect the console directly to the display.
  • Visual corruption in games (Xbox). Xbox Wire’s advice from 2020: untick Allow variable refresh rate, or set the refresh rate to 60 Hz if you prefer VRR over 120 Hz.
  • VRR on, but no difference. The game may run at a locked frame rate that matches the refresh rate, or below your display’s VRR window.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need HDMI 2.1 for 120 Hz on PS5 and Xbox?

For 4K at 120 Hz, yes: Sony and Microsoft ask for an HDMI 2.1 port and a suitable cable. Xbox Support also describes 120 Hz at lower resolutions on a TV without HDMI 2.1.

Does VRR increase input lag?

Sony’s announcement says VRR reduces input lag in many PS5 games, with results that vary by game, TV and visual mode. VRR avoids waiting for a fixed refresh, which is where V-Sync adds delay.

Why does my 30 fps mode not benefit from VRR?

VRR follows the frame rate only inside your display’s window. If the window starts above 30 Hz and the frames are not doubled, a steady 30 fps gains little.

Which HDMI cable do I need?

The one supplied with the console, or an Ultra High Speed HDMI cable rated for 4K 120 Hz, as Sony and Microsoft describe it.
