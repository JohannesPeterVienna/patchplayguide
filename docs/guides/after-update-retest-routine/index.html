After a big update: a 10-minute re-test routine

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 2, 20264 min readHow we check pages

A large game update or a new graphics driver changes more than the patch notes say: settings can be reset, presets can move, and the shader cache is usually rebuilt. Judging the update in its first minutes leads to wrong conclusions. This routine takes about ten minutes and tells you whether something really changed for you – and what to do about it.

Before the update: two minutes of notes

The routine works best if you have a reference. Before you install a big update – or right now, for the next one – write down or photograph:

  • Your graphics or mode settings (a photo of each menu page is fastest).
  • Your test scene or route and its average FPS and 1% lows, or on console how the mode looked and felt.
  • The game version and, on PC, the graphics driver version.

If you have no notes, the routine still works: you compare against your memory of how the game ran, and you create the notes for next time.

The routine

10-minute re-test routine
MinuteStepWhy
0–1Read the patch notes for your platform; paste them into the Patch DecoderKnow what to look for: presets, modes, shaders, known issues
1–2Check every settings page against your notes or photosUpdates can reset or move settings silently
2–5Let shader compilation finish; play calmly through your test area onceThe first run measures compilation, not the game
5–8Measure your test route twice (or watch it closely on console)Two runs show whether the result is stable
8–10Compare with your notes; decide on one change if neededChange one thing at a time

The Patch Decoder builds a checklist with these base steps plus the actions from the patch lines that concern you, and exports it as text – handy to keep with your test notes.

Reading the result

Same as before. Done. Keep the notes; they are your reference for the next update.

Better. If the update optimized something you had lowered – for example a CPU improvement in crowded areas – try that option one step higher and test again.

Worse. Go through the causes in order: Was the shader cache still being built (does a second run look better)? Did a setting change? Is there a known issue for your platform? Only if all three are ruled out is it likely that the update made the game heavier. Then find your current bottleneck with the Bottleneck Finder and lower one option for it, using the Settings Translator.

On consoles

Consoles have no frame counter in most games, but the routine still works. Check that the mode you chose (performance, quality or fidelity, 120 Hz, VRR) is still selected, both in the game and in the console’s system settings. Then play your usual area and pay attention to smoothness and sharpness. If a patch note says a mode’s target changed, compare both modes again – the best choice may have changed. The guides on PS5 modes, Xbox Series S vs X and Switch 2 explain the trade-offs.

When to report a problem

If you have confirmed a clear drop under the same conditions, report it to the developer through their official support or bug-report channel. Include your platform, hardware, game and driver version, the scene, your before-and-after numbers and the settings. Reports with numbers and steps to reproduce are far more useful than “performance is worse”.

And keep the result in perspective: after an update it is normal for the first session to feel slightly different. Patience for one session, plus a short structured test, avoids most unnecessary changes – and most of the frustration.

Driver updates follow the same rules

Graphics driver updates behave like game updates for this routine: the shader cache is rebuilt, defaults in the driver control panel can change, and performance in individual games can shift in either direction. Install drivers when you have time for a calm first session, read the driver’s release notes for your games, and use the same re-test.

A personal reference sheet

The routine gets faster each time if you keep one small note per game. It does not need to be elaborate – a text file or a phone note is enough:

  • Game, platform, game version and (PC) driver version.
  • Settings or mode, with a photo of each menu page.
  • Test route: where it starts, where it ends, what you do.
  • Results: average FPS and 1% lows (PC), or a one-line impression (console).
  • Date, and what changed since the last entry.

The Patch Decoder’s text export fits right below it. After a few updates you have a history that shows whether a game is getting better or worse on your system – far more useful than any general claim.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a game run worse right after an update?

Most often because the shader cache is rebuilt, so the first session stutters. Settings may also have been reset. Re-test after a calm first play-through.

How long should I wait before judging performance?

At least until any shader compilation step has finished and you have played once through your test area. Then measure.

Do I need special software for the routine?

No. A frame counter is enough on PC; on console you compare by eye. Frame-time tools give more precise numbers if you want them.

Should I reinstall the game if it runs worse?

Rarely. Check shaders, settings and known issues first. A reinstall is a last resort for corrupted files and makes the next session stutter again.

Sources and further reading

  • Official patch notes of your game – publisher website, store news page or in-game news
  • Graphics driver release notes – published by NVIDIA, AMD and Intel with each driver
  • PresentMon – frame-time capture for before/after comparisons

Sources without a link are named so you can find the current version yourself; vendor pages move often. We summarize in our own words and never copy patch notes.
