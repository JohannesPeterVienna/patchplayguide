Ray tracing settings explained: what to keep, what to drop

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 2, 20264 min readHow we check pages

Ray tracing is not one setting but a family of them: reflections, shadows, ambient occlusion, global illumination and, at the top, full path tracing. Some change the look of a scene completely; others are hard to spot. Since ray tracing is among the most expensive things a game can do, knowing which effect is worth its cost makes a large difference.

How ray tracing differs from traditional rendering

Traditional rendering (rasterization) projects triangles onto the screen and uses clever approximations for light: shadow maps for shadows, screen-space techniques for reflections and ambient occlusion, and pre-calculated or probe-based lighting for indirect light. These approximations are fast, but they break in typical places – reflections disappear when the reflected object leaves the screen, and indirect light does not react to changes in the scene.

Ray tracing follows rays of light through a 3D description of the scene. To do that quickly, the GPU needs an acceleration structure (often a bounding volume hierarchy) that has to be built and updated as things move – work that costs GPU time, VRAM and also some CPU time. Each ray then has to be tested against that structure, and the noisy result has to be cleaned up by a denoiser. Graphics cards with dedicated ray-tracing hardware do this much faster than those without.

Costs are general tendencies across current games, not measurements. Individual implementations vary widely.

A good rule: global illumination and reflections are the effects most likely to change how a game looks; ray-traced shadows are often the hardest to tell apart from good traditional shadows. If you want ray tracing but need performance, drop RT shadows first and keep GI or reflections – depending on the game.

Why it costs so much – and what helps

The number of rays scales with the number of pixels, which is why ray tracing and upscaling go together: at the Quality mode of an upscaler, the game traces rays for less than half the pixels. Some upscalers replace the game’s denoiser with a trained model (NVIDIA calls this Ray Reconstruction), which can improve the stability of ray-traced effects.

Ray tracing quality presets usually change rays per pixel, the number of light bounces, the distance up to which ray-traced effects are used and the denoiser quality. One step down from the maximum is often hard to see and saves a lot.

Ray tracing and video memory

Acceleration structures and extra buffers need memory, often a significant amount on top of textures. On cards with limited VRAM, enabling ray tracing can push a game over the limit, which shows up as stutter or blurry textures rather than as a lower average frame rate. If that happens, lower textures one step or drop ray tracing – see VRAM full: what to lower first.

How to decide

  • Get a stable base frame rate first with an upscaler; ray tracing on top of native resolution is rarely affordable.
  • Turn effects on one at a time and compare the same scene with and without. Keep the one that changes the look most.
  • For fast multiplayer games, prefer frame rate over ray tracing.
  • For slow, atmospheric single-player games, ray-traced GI or reflections can be worth a lower frame rate.
  • On consoles, ray tracing is usually part of the fidelity mode – see PS5 modes.

The Settings Translator rates ray-traced lighting and reflections as high GPU and moderate VRAM load. Patches regularly change ray tracing – new modes, a new denoiser, better performance – so re-test after updates that mention it.

Hybrid and software ray tracing

Some engines use ray tracing in software or in a hybrid form – for example tracing against simplified scene representations – so that GPUs without fast ray-tracing hardware can still get some of the benefit. In such games, “hardware ray tracing” is often an extra option that improves quality further at higher cost. Read the game’s own description of each option; names are not standardized.

Ray tracing and game updates

Ray tracing is one of the areas developers change most after launch: new modes are added, denoisers are replaced, performance improves, or ray tracing arrives on consoles in a later patch. When the Patch Decoder files a line under graphics with a ray-tracing term, compare the same scene before and after – both how it looks and how it runs. An improved denoiser can make a lower RT quality setting look as good as the higher one did before the patch.

Frequently asked questions

Which ray tracing setting should I turn off first?

Usually ray-traced shadows, because they are often the hardest to distinguish from good traditional shadows. Keep global illumination or reflections if the game uses them well.

Does ray tracing need a lot of VRAM?

It adds memory for acceleration structures and buffers. On cards with limited VRAM, it can push a game over the limit and cause stutter.

Is path tracing the same as ray tracing?

Path tracing is the most complete form of ray tracing: all lighting is computed with rays and multiple bounces. It is far more demanding than individual ray-traced effects.

Why does ray tracing look noisy or smeared?

Games trace only a few rays per pixel and rely on denoisers and temporal accumulation, which can smear or flicker in motion. Higher RT quality or a better denoiser reduces it.
