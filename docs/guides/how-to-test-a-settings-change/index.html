How to test a settings change – and re-test after an update

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 18, 20268 min readHow we check pages

Most “this setting gives +20 FPS” claims come from a single glance at a counter. Frame rates jump around by several percent from one second to the next, so a single reading can easily show a gain that is not there. A proper comparison takes about ten minutes, needs no paid tools, and gives you numbers you can compare again after the next patch – which is exactly when you need them. The second half of this guide is the short version for the first session after a game or driver update.

Four principles

  • Same scene. Compare in exactly the same place, ideally with the same movement. Two different views of the same level can differ more than any setting.
  • One change at a time. If you change shadows and textures together, you cannot tell which one mattered.
  • Warm up first. The first run after starting the game – and especially after an update – includes shader compilation and loading. Do not measure it.
  • Several runs. Measure each setting at least three times and compare the middle values. With fewer runs, one odd run decides the result.

These principles apply to PC and handhelds, where you can change single options. On consoles, the same method works for comparing modes, using your eyes and the game’s own information instead of a counter.

Choose a good test scene

A good test scene is demanding, repeatable and typical of how you play. Built-in benchmarks are convenient and repeatable, but some do not represent the heaviest gameplay – a city full of characters may stress the CPU far more than the benchmark’s flyover. A short route of 30 to 60 seconds that you can walk the same way every time is often more useful.

Write the scene down: location, time of day in the game if it changes, weather, route. Then you can repeat the test after the next update, when the re-test routine below asks for your old numbers.

What to measure

Record at least average FPS and 1% lows – the frame rate during the slowest one percent of frames, which captures stutter that the average hides. Tools such as PresentMon, CapFrameX or the overlays of GPU driver software can record a run and report both. If you only have a counter, write down the typical value and the lowest value you see during the route – less precise, but still useful.

Also note the conditions: game version, driver version, resolution, upscaler mode and the full preset. A number without its conditions cannot be compared later. Our guide to FPS, frame time and 1% lows explains how these figures are calculated and why different tools give slightly different 1% values.

The routine, step by step

  1. Start the game, load your save and walk the route once without measuring (warm-up).
  2. Measure the route with your current settings: run A1.
  3. Change one setting. Apply it; restart the game if the menu asks for it.
  4. Walk the route once without measuring, then measure: run B1.
  5. Switch back and measure A2, switch again and measure B2, and once more for A3 and B3. Alternating spreads slow drifts – heat, background tasks – evenly over both settings.
  6. Compare the middle value (median) of the three A runs with that of the three B runs, for both average FPS and 1% lows.

When is a difference real?

Look first at how much the runs of the same setting differ from each other – that spread is your noise. A difference between two settings means something only when it stands clear of it. The simplest strict rule: call a difference real only when every run of one setting is faster than every run of the other, so that the two ranges do not overlap.

A worked example with invented numbers shows how it reads. Shadows on High give 71, 73 and 72 fps in three runs; shadows on Medium give 75, 74 and 77 fps.

Invented example: three runs per setting (not measurements)
A · Shadows HighB · Shadows Medium
Runs (average FPS)71 · 73 · 7275 · 74 · 77
Median72 fps75 fps
Lowest–highest71–73 (spread 2 fps)74–77 (spread 3 fps)
Frame time at the median13.89 ms13.33 ms

The slowest B run (74) beats the fastest A run (73), so the ranges do not overlap: the gain of 3 fps, about 4%, is real in this scene. Had one A run reached 75, the ranges would overlap and the honest answer would be “not clear – more runs or a longer route”.

The rule has a known error rate, which is why three runs are the minimum. If a setting changed nothing, the six runs would be six draws from the same pot; of the 20 ways to split them into two groups of three, only 2 put all runs of one group on top. So pure chance produces a “clear” result in 1 of 10 such tests. With five runs per setting it is 1 of 126. When a decision matters – a setting you would otherwise leave on – do five.

Then check whether the difference matters. In the example the frame time drops by 0.56 ms; that is real but small. The same 3 fps from 57 to 60 on a 60 Hz display without VRR can be the difference between even and uneven motion. Improvements in 1% lows are often more noticeable than in the average.

Finally, look at the image. Take a screenshot at the same spot with each setting and compare them side by side, then decide whether the visual difference is worth the performance difference. The Settings Translator suggests which options are worth testing first for your bottleneck.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring right after an update or driver install – you are measuring shader compilation.
  • Comparing different scenes, or day and night in a game with a day cycle.
  • Testing with a frame cap or V-Sync active – both settings may hit the cap and look identical.
  • Laptop on battery or in a quiet mode for one run and plugged in for the other.
  • Trusting one run because the result is the one you hoped for.

After a big update: the 10-minute version

A large game update or a new graphics driver changes more than the patch notes say: settings can be reset, presets can move, and the shader cache is usually rebuilt. Judging the update in its first minutes leads to wrong conclusions. The same method, shortened, tells you whether something really changed for you.

10-minute re-test routine
MinuteStepWhy
0–1Read the patch notes for your platform; paste them into the Patch DecoderKnow what to look for: presets, modes, shaders, known issues
1–2Check every settings page against your notes or photosUpdates can reset or move settings silently
2–5Let shader compilation finish; play calmly through your test area onceThe first run measures compilation, not the game
5–8Measure your test route three times (or watch it closely on console)Three runs show the spread
8–10Compare with your notes; decide on one change if neededChange one thing at a time

Same as before – that is, your new runs overlap with the old ones: done. Better: if the update optimized something you had lowered, try that option one step higher and test again. Worse: go through the causes in order. Was the shader cache still being built (does a second pass look better – see the two-pass test)? Did a setting change? Is there a known issue for your platform? Only if all three are ruled out is it likely that the update made the game heavier. Then find your current bottleneck with the Bottleneck Finder and lower one option for it.

Graphics driver updates follow the same rules: the shader cache is rebuilt, defaults in the driver’s control panel can change, and performance in individual games can shift in either direction.

On consoles there is usually no frame counter, but the routine still works. Check that the mode you chose (performance, quality, 120 Hz, VRR) is still selected, both in the game and in the console’s system settings, then play your usual area and pay attention to smoothness and sharpness. If a patch note says a mode’s target changed, compare both modes again.

Notes that make the next test fast

The routine only works with a reference. Before you install a big update – or right now, for the next one – keep one small note per game:

  • Game, platform, game version and (on PC) the graphics driver version.
  • Your settings or mode; a photo of each menu page is fastest.
  • The test route: where it starts, where it ends, what you do.
  • Results: the three runs with average FPS and 1% lows (PC), or a one-line impression (console).
  • Date, and what changed since the last entry.

The printable worksheet of the Settings Test Log has exactly these fields, and the log’s summary or CSV export can be pasted into a text file. After a few updates you have a history that shows whether a game is getting better or worse on your own system.

When to report a problem. If you have confirmed a clear drop under the same conditions, report it to the developer through their official support or bug-report channel. Include platform, hardware, game and driver version, the scene, your before-and-after runs and the settings. A report with numbers and steps to reproduce is far more useful than “performance is worse”.

Frequently asked questions

How many test runs do I need?

At least three per setting, after a warm-up run. With three runs each, a clean split between the two settings still appears by chance in 1 of 10 tests; with five runs each, in 1 of 126.

Are built-in benchmarks reliable?

They are very repeatable, which is good for comparisons. They do not always represent the heaviest gameplay, especially CPU-heavy scenes with many characters.

Should I disable the frame cap for testing?

Yes, for performance comparisons. With a cap, both settings may hit the limit and look identical even when one is much faster.

Why does a game run worse right after an update?

Most often because the shader cache is rebuilt, so the first session stutters. Settings may also have been reset. Re-test after a calm first play-through.

Should I reinstall the game if it runs worse after an update?

Rarely. Check shaders, settings and known issues first. A reinstall is a last resort for corrupted files and makes the next session stutter again.
