How to test a settings change properly

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 2, 20264 min readHow we check pages

Most “this setting gives +20 FPS” claims come from a single glance at a counter. Frame rates jump around by several percent from one second to the next, so a single reading can easily show a gain that is not there. A proper comparison takes about ten minutes, needs no paid tools, and gives you numbers you can trust and compare after the next patch.

Four principles

  • Same scene. Compare in exactly the same place, ideally with the same movement. Two different views of the same level can differ more than any setting.
  • One change at a time. If you change shadows and textures together, you cannot tell which one mattered.
  • Warm up first. The first run after starting the game – and especially after an update – includes shader compilation and loading. Do not measure it.
  • Several runs. Measure each setting at least twice, better three times, and compare averages.

These principles apply to PC and handhelds, where you can change single options. On consoles, the same method works for comparing modes, using your eyes and the game’s own information instead of a counter.

Choose a good test scene

A good test scene is demanding, repeatable and typical of how you play. Built-in benchmarks are convenient and repeatable, but some do not represent the heaviest gameplay – a city full of characters may stress the CPU far more than the benchmark’s flyover. A short route of 30 to 60 seconds that you can walk the same way every time is often more useful.

Write the scene down: location, time of day in the game if it changes, weather, route. Then you can repeat the test after the next update, when the re-test routine asks for your old numbers.

What to measure

Record at least average FPS and 1% lows – the frame rate during the slowest one percent of frames, which captures stutter that the average hides. Tools such as PresentMon, CapFrameX or the overlays of GPU driver software can record a run and report both. If you only have a counter, write down the typical value and the lowest value you see during the route – less precise, but still useful.

Also note the conditions: game version, driver version, resolution, upscaler mode and the full preset. A number without its conditions cannot be compared later. Our guide to FPS, frame time and 1% lows explains how these figures are calculated and why different tools give slightly different 1% values.

The routine, step by step

  1. Start the game, load your save and walk the route once without measuring (warm-up).
  2. Measure the route with your current settings: run A1.
  3. Change one setting. Apply it; restart the game if the menu asks for it.
  4. Walk the route once without measuring, then measure: run B1.
  5. Switch back and measure A2, then switch again and measure B2. Alternating reduces the effect of the system warming up.
  6. Compare the averages of A and B, for both average FPS and 1% lows.

When is a difference real?

Look at how much the runs of the same setting differ from each other. If A1 and A2 differ by 3%, a 2% difference between A and B is noise. A useful rule: treat a difference as real only when it is clearly larger than the spread between repeated runs of the same setting – and when it points the same way in every pair.

Also check whether it matters. A 3% gain from 140 to 144 FPS is invisible to most people; the same 3% from 58 to 60 FPS on a 60 Hz display can be the difference between smooth and uneven motion. Improvements in 1% lows are often more noticeable than in the average.

Finally, look at the image. Take a screenshot at the same spot with each setting and compare them side by side, then decide whether the visual difference is worth the performance difference. The Settings Translator suggests which options are worth testing first for your bottleneck.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring right after an update or driver install – you are measuring shader compilation.
  • Comparing different scenes, or day and night in a game with a day cycle.
  • Testing with a frame cap or V-Sync active – both settings may hit the cap and look identical.
  • Laptop on battery or in a quiet mode for one run and plugged in for the other.
  • Trusting one run because the result is the one you hoped for.

Frequently asked questions

How many test runs do I need?

At least two per setting, better three, after a warm-up run. Alternate the settings so slow changes affect both equally.

Are built-in benchmarks reliable?

They are very repeatable, which is good for comparisons. They do not always represent the heaviest gameplay, especially CPU-heavy scenes with many characters.

What are 1% lows?

The frame rate during the slowest one percent of frames in a run. They show stutter and hitches that the average hides.

Should I disable the frame cap for testing?

Yes, for performance comparisons. With a cap, both settings may hit the limit and look identical even when one is much faster.
