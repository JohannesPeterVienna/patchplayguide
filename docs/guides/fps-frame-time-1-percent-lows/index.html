FPS, frame time and 1% lows explained

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 2, 20264 min readHow we check pages

Average FPS is the number everybody quotes, and the one that tells you least about how smooth a game feels. Two runs with the same average can feel completely different if one of them has regular hitches. Frame time and 1% lows show what the average hides. This guide explains the numbers, how tools calculate them and how to read a frame-time graph.

Frame time: the number behind FPS

Frame time is how long one frame takes, in milliseconds. It is the inverse of the frame rate: frame time = 1000 ÷ FPS. At 60 fps each frame takes 16.7 ms on average, at 120 fps 8.3 ms, at 30 fps 33.3 ms. Frame time is the better unit for comparisons because it is linear in effort: saving 5 ms means the same amount of work saved whether you start at 30 or at 60 fps, while the FPS change looks very different.

Why the average hides stutter

Average FPS is the number of frames divided by the duration of the run. Imagine one second with 59 frames of 16 ms and one frame of 56 ms: the average is 60 fps, but that one long frame is a visible hitch. Repeat it every second and the game stutters constantly while the counter still says 60. This is why reviewers and careful testers report low percentiles alongside the average.

What 1% and 0.1% lows are

The 1% low describes the slowest one percent of frames in a run. There are two common ways to calculate it, and tools differ:

  • Percentile method: take the 99th percentile frame time – the frame time that 99% of frames are faster than – and convert it to FPS.
  • Average-of-the-worst method: take the slowest 1% of frames, average their frame times, and convert to FPS.

The second method is more sensitive to individual extreme spikes and usually gives a lower number. Some tools also weight frames by time rather than counting them. The 0.1% low works the same way for the slowest 0.1% of frames and highlights rare, large hitches – but in a short run it is based on very few frames and fluctuates a lot. The practical consequence: compare 1% lows only from the same tool with the same method, and use runs long enough to contain a few thousand frames.

Reading a frame-time graph

  • A flat line means even pacing – the ideal, regardless of its height.
  • Single tall spikes are hitches: shader compilation, asset streaming, a background task. If they appear only on the first run, think shaders.
  • A regular sawtooth or alternating pattern suggests frame pacing issues, often with V-Sync or a mismatched cap.
  • A higher, noisy band in certain areas means those scenes are heavier – often a CPU limit in crowded places.

Tools such as PresentMon, CapFrameX and the overlays from GPU vendors show the graph live or record it for later analysis. On Steam Deck, the performance overlay at a higher level shows a frame-time graph as well.

What matters for how a game feels

Smoothness depends on even frame times and on how they line up with the display. A steady 40 fps on a 40 Hz Steam Deck screen can feel better than an uneven 50 fps on a 60 Hz screen. A VRR display tolerates fluctuations within its range, but no display hides a 100 ms hitch. Responsiveness depends on the frame rate the game actually renders plus any queuing – see frame caps, V-Sync and latency. When you compare settings, look at both average FPS and 1% lows, as described in how to test a settings change.

Common misreadings

Comparing an average from one tool with a 1% low from another; judging from a run of a few seconds; comparing runs where one was capped; and forgetting that the first run after an update includes shader compilation. Each of these can make a setting look better or worse than it is. Write down the tool, method and conditions next to every number.

Frame-time targets for common displays

A useful way to think about smoothness is the frame-time budget of your display. On a 60 Hz display each refresh lasts 16.7 ms; to show a new frame every refresh, every frame has to fit in that budget. On 120 Hz the budget is 8.3 ms, on 144 Hz 6.9 ms. Frames that exceed the budget cause a missed refresh without VRR, or a longer refresh with VRR. That is why 1% lows matter more as refresh rates rise: the budget shrinks, and occasional long frames become more visible against a very short normal frame time.

Frequently asked questions

What are good 1% lows?

There is no universal number. What matters is how close they are to the average: lows far below the average mean noticeable stutter. Compare them between settings in the same scene and tool.

Why do different tools show different 1% lows?

They calculate them differently – as a percentile or as the average of the slowest frames – and may weight frames differently. Only compare numbers from the same tool.

How do I convert FPS to frame time?

Divide 1000 by the frame rate: 60 fps is 16.7 ms per frame, 120 fps is 8.3 ms.

Is a higher average FPS always better?

Not if it comes with worse frame pacing. An even, slightly lower frame rate often feels smoother than a higher, uneven one.
