Steam Deck: frame cap, TDP and battery basics

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 24, 20267 min readHow we check pages

The Steam Deck gives you more control than a console: a Performance tab with frame limit, refresh rate, power limit and scaling options, saved per game if you want. Used together, they decide how evenly a game runs and how long the battery lasts. This guide explains the options, the 40 Hz setting Valve itself calls a sweet spot, the frame-rate data Steam has shown since November 2026, and the battery arithmetic.

Where the settings are

Press the … (Quick Access) button and open the Performance tab (the battery icon). Turn on “Use per-game profile” to store the settings for the game you are playing instead of globally. The option names below are those of the Performance tab; Valve documents the refresh-rate and frame-limit settings in its SteamOS 3.2 notes, and the hardware limits in its tech specs. The descriptions of the other options are our explanation, and the layout can change with SteamOS updates.

Frame cap first

An uncapped game runs as fast as it can, so the frame rate swings with the scene and the APU stays at full power. A cap that the game holds almost everywhere gives even frame pacing and leaves power unused.

The 40 fps setting. Set the refresh rate to 40 Hz and cap at 40 fps. Each frame then takes 25 ms, exactly halfway between 30 fps (33.3 ms) and 60 fps (16.7 ms), and it fits the refresh rate evenly. Valve’s own SteamOS 3.2 notes say the same: 40 Hz “tends to be a sweet spot for responsiveness, consistency, and smoothness”, and a rock-solid 40 fps at 40 Hz can feel smoother than a frame rate that swings between 42 and 48 on a 60 Hz screen. On the OLED model with its 90 Hz screen, 45 fps at 90 Hz is another even combination (arithmetic: 90 ÷ 45 = 2).

Check Steam’s frame-rate data before you tune

Since November 10, 2026, Steam shows the average frame rate that players get in a game on Steam Deck, with a histogram of how often each frame rate occurs. Valve collects it from players who opted in to sharing anonymous frame-rate data, only from Decks set to the default frame limit – 60 fps on the LCD model, 90 fps on the OLED model – and without accounting for in-game settings. It is on each game’s store page under Steam Deck Compatibility → Learn more, for games with a Verified, Playable or Unsupported rating and enough data.

Two things make it useful for tuning. The average tells you before you start whether 60, 45 or 40 fps is realistic for a game. And the histogram shows the effect of V-Sync that this guide is about: Valve explains that gaps such as 90 and 45 fps with nothing in between appear when a game misses the refresh rate and V-Sync drops it to half – on a 90 Hz screen, a game averaging 75 fps alternates between 90 and 45. A cap that the game can hold avoids exactly that.

Resolution and upscaling

The Steam Deck screen is 1280×800, and Valve lists 16 GB of LPDDR5 on-board memory for both models. If a game has its own upscaler – FSR, XeSS or an engine upscaler – use it with the output at 1280×800. Alternatively, set the game to a lower resolution such as 960×600 and use the Steam Deck’s FSR scaling filter. Which effects to lower first is an editorial estimate in the Settings Translator; on the Deck our suggestion is to start with resolution and the frame cap, because CPU and GPU share one power budget (4–15 W).

TDP and battery

The TDP limit caps how much power the APU may use; Valve’s specs give a range of 4 to 15 W. With a frame cap in place, a game may not need the full budget: lower the limit until the frame rate starts to drop, then raise it a step. CPU and GPU share the budget, so a limit that is too low slows both and can cause stutter in CPU-heavy moments.

  • Set the frame cap first, then lower the TDP while watching the overlay.
  • Screen brightness also draws power – lower it indoors.
  • Use per-game profiles so a light game does not inherit the settings of a demanding one.
  • Check the Steam Deck Compatibility rating (Verified, Playable, Unsupported) and, since November 2026, the average frame rate on the store page.

Worked example: battery arithmetic

Battery life is a division: capacity in watt-hours divided by the power the whole device draws in watts. Valve’s tech specs give the capacities – 40 Whr for the LCD model and 50 Whr for the OLED model – and the performance overlay shows the current draw while you play.

Runtime = battery capacity ÷ total power draw (calculated)
Total draw shown in the overlayLCD model, 40 WhrOLED model, 50 Whr
25 W1.6 h2.0 h
20 W2.0 h2.5 h
15 W2.7 h3.3 h
10 W4.0 h5.0 h
7 W5.7 h7.1 h

Pure arithmetic from Valve’s capacity figures. A real session ends a little earlier, because a battery is not drained to zero and the draw changes from scene to scene.

Valve states 2 to 8 hours of gameplay for the LCD model and 3 to 12 hours for the OLED model. Run through the same division, those ranges correspond to an average draw between 20 W and 5 W on the LCD model and between about 17 W and 4 W on the OLED model. The whole spread between a short and a long session is the power draw, and that is what a frame cap, a lower TDP limit and a dimmer screen change. Read the draw in the overlay before and after a change and you can calculate what it bought you: going from 20 W to 12 W on the LCD model turns 2.0 hours into 3.3.

The same arithmetic applies to resolution. The 1280×800 screen has 1.02 megapixels. FSR at Quality (1.5× per axis) renders 853×533, which is 44% of the pixels; Performance (2.0×) renders 640×400, a quarter. At such low pixel counts the upscaler has little to work with – our suggestion is a higher upscaler mode with a lower frame cap rather than a low mode at a high frame rate.

A simple order for any game

  1. Look up the game’s average frame rate on its store page, if Steam shows one.
  2. Start with the game’s medium or “Steam Deck” preset if it has one, and turn on the performance overlay.
  3. Choose a cap the game can hold nearly always: 30, 40 (with 40 Hz), 45 (OLED, 90 Hz) or 60.
  4. If it cannot hold the cap, lower the render resolution or use an upscaler before lowering textures.
  5. Lower the TDP limit for battery if the frame rate stays stable, and save the per-game profile.

After a game update, check that the profile is still active and expect some shader compilation – see shader stutter after an update.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best frame cap on Steam Deck?

The highest one your game holds nearly all the time. Valve calls 40 Hz a sweet spot for responsiveness, consistency and smoothness; lighter games can run at 60, and the OLED model also allows 45 fps at 90 Hz.

Where can I see how well a game runs on Steam Deck?

Since November 10, 2026, on the game’s store page under Steam Deck Compatibility → Learn more: Steam shows the average frame rate of players who share anonymous data, measured at the default frame limit.

Does lowering TDP improve battery life?

Yes, if the game still holds its frame cap, because the battery lasts capacity ÷ power draw. Lower it gradually while watching the overlay; too low a limit causes drops and stutter.

Should I use the FSR scaling filter?

It helps when a game renders below 1280×800 and has no built-in upscaler. If the game offers its own upscaler, our suggestion is to try that first, because it works with the game’s own data.
