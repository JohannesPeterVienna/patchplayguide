DLSS vs FSR vs XeSS: which upscaler mode to pick

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 2, 20264 min readHow we check pages

Upscalers render the game at a lower internal resolution and reconstruct a sharper image for your display. Used well, they are the cheapest way to gain GPU performance. The names – DLSS, FSR, XeSS, TSR, PSSR – and the modes from Quality to Ultra Performance hide a simple question: how many pixels does the game really render, and how good is the reconstruction?

What an upscaler does

A temporal upscaler renders each frame at a reduced resolution, with a tiny camera offset that changes from frame to frame. It then combines information from the current and previous frames, guided by motion vectors from the game, to rebuild detail at the output resolution. Because it accumulates samples over time, the result can look close to native resolution – and it replaces the game’s own anti-aliasing.

The quality depends on how the reconstruction decides which old information to keep. Machine-learning upscalers use a trained neural network for that decision; others use hand-written rules. Errors show up as ghosting behind moving objects, shimmering on fine detail such as fences and foliage, or softness in motion.

These ratios are the commonly documented defaults for DLSS and FSR. XeSS uses similar mode names, but its ratios have changed between versions, and games may use their own values or a free slider – check the internal resolution the game shows.

Two lessons follow from the table. First, even “Quality” renders less than half the pixels of native resolution, which is why it often gains a lot of performance. Second, the output resolution matters: Performance mode at 4K renders a full 1080p image and usually looks good; Performance mode at 1080p output renders only 960×540 and looks noticeably softer. The lower your display resolution, the higher the mode you should pick.

As a rule of thumb, the machine-learning upscalers tend to hold fine detail and stability better at aggressive modes, while FSR 2 and 3 upscaling run almost everywhere. Results vary by game, because each integration and its motion vectors differ. If your card supports several, try them in the same scene: walk past foliage and fences and look for shimmer and ghosting.

Which mode to pick

  • Set the display’s native resolution as output; never lower the output resolution when an upscaler is available.
  • Start with Quality. If you need more frames, step to Balanced, then Performance – one step at a time, testing each.
  • If the image looks soft, a sharpening slider can help; too much sharpening creates halos and shimmering.
  • Prefer an upscaler mode over lowering many effects: it usually costs less visible quality for the same gain.
  • If you are CPU-bound, upscaling gains little – the GPU was not the limit. See CPU or GPU bottleneck?

Upscalers and game updates

Game updates often change upscalers: a new DLSS, FSR or XeSS version, a new default mode, a sharpening change or a fix for ghosting. The same mode name can then look or perform differently. When the Patch Decoder sorts an upscaling line as a change (~), compare your current mode in a fixed scene before and after. On consoles, the developer chooses the upscaler; you choose the mode – see PS5 performance vs fidelity modes.

How to compare upscalers yourself

Comparisons in videos are compressed and rarely show your game at your resolution. A two-minute comparison of your own is more reliable. Pick a scene with fine, high-contrast detail – a chain-link fence, power lines, grass or hair – and some movement, ideally a character running past a detailed background. Then switch between the available upscalers at the same mode and look for four things:

  • Stability: do thin lines shimmer or crawl while the camera moves slowly?
  • Ghosting: do moving objects leave a faint trail, especially against the sky?
  • Sharpness in motion: does the image blur when you turn and sharpen again when you stop?
  • Particles and transparency: do sparks, rain or smoke break up or smear?

Note the frame rate of each option as well – upscalers have a small cost of their own, and the machine-learning ones can cost slightly more on some cards. If two options look equal to you, take the faster one; if one is clearly more stable, a slightly lower frame rate is usually worth it. Re-check after updates that mention a new upscaler version.

Frequently asked questions

Is DLSS Quality better than native resolution?

Sometimes it looks comparable or more stable than native with a weak anti-aliasing method, sometimes slightly softer. It depends on the game and the output resolution; compare in a scene with fine detail.

Can I use FSR on an NVIDIA card?

FSR 2 and FSR 3 upscaling are designed to run on graphics cards from all major vendors, so yes, if the game offers it. FSR 4 targets recent Radeon cards.

Which upscaler mode for 1080p?

Quality is the usual choice. Lower modes render very few pixels at 1080p output and tend to look soft.

Does upscaling help if I am CPU-bound?

Very little. Upscaling reduces GPU work; if the processor is the limit, the frame rate hardly changes.
