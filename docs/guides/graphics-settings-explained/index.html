Graphics settings explained: what each option costs

By the Patch Play Guide editorial team, AdStern

Reviewed November 2, 20265 min readHow we check pages

A modern graphics menu has twenty or more options, and most of them barely change the frame rate. A few do almost all the work. This guide explains what each common option does, which part of your PC pays for it – graphics card, processor or video memory – and in which order to lower them when you need more performance.

Three resources pay for every frame

Each frame is prepared by the CPU, which runs the game logic, moves characters, simulates physics and tells the graphics card what to draw. The GPU then draws the image: it shades every pixel, calculates light and shadow, and applies effects. Everything the GPU needs quickly – textures, geometry, intermediate images – sits in its video memory (VRAM).

The frame rate is set by whichever stage is slowest. That is the key to settings: an option only helps if it relieves the stage that is currently the limit. Lowering shadows on a PC whose processor is the bottleneck costs image quality and gains nothing. If you do not know your limit yet, run the Bottleneck Finder or the 5-minute check first.

Resolution and upscaling: the biggest lever

The number of pixels the game renders is the strongest single factor for GPU load. 4K has four times the pixels of 1080p and 2.25 times those of 1440p. Because most per-pixel work scales with that count, lowering the render resolution is the most reliable way to gain GPU performance.

Lowering the output resolution of a flat-panel display makes the image soft, because the screen has to scale it. The better way is to keep the output at the display’s native resolution and lower the internal resolution with an upscaler – DLSS, FSR, XeSS or the engine’s own temporal upscaler – or with a render-scale slider. Modern upscalers reconstruct detail from previous frames and usually look much better than simple scaling. Our upscaler guide explains the modes.

Shadows, lighting and effects

Shadow quality controls the resolution, distance and softness of shadows. It loads the GPU and, in many engines, the CPU too, because more objects have to be drawn into the shadow maps. The top step is often expensive and hard to see in motion, so one step below the maximum is a classic low-loss gain.

Ambient occlusion darkens corners and contact points so objects look grounded. Methods differ in cost; a cheaper method usually looks fine. Volumetric effects – fog, god rays, clouds – can be surprisingly expensive at the highest preset, and the difference to the step below is often small. Screen-space reflections reuse what is already on screen; ray-traced reflections and lighting are much more accurate and much more expensive, see ray tracing settings explained.

Particle and effects quality matters mostly in heavy moments – explosions, spells, smoke – and is a good candidate when the frame rate collapses only in big fights.

Textures and filtering

Texture quality sets the resolution of the images on surfaces. As long as they fit into video memory, higher textures cost little GPU time and improve the image clearly, so they are the last thing to lower on a card with enough VRAM. When memory runs full, the picture changes: textures load late or look blurry, and stutter appears because data has to be swapped over the slower connection to system memory. Then texture quality becomes the first option to lower – see VRAM full: what to lower first.

Anisotropic filtering keeps textures sharp on surfaces seen at a shallow angle, such as roads and floors. On current graphics cards it typically costs very little, so 8x or 16x is usually a safe choice.

World detail: what the CPU pays for

View distance (or draw distance, level of detail) decides how far away objects appear in full detail. Crowd, traffic and NPC density decides how many characters and vehicles are simulated. Physics and destruction decide how many objects move realistically. All three mostly load the CPU, because every object has to be updated and submitted for drawing each frame.

These options are the first to lower when you are CPU-bound, typically in large open worlds, cities and multiplayer matches with many players. On a GPU-bound system they help little. Foliage density sits in between: grass and trees cost both CPU submission work and GPU time.

Anti-aliasing and post-processing

Anti-aliasing smooths jagged edges. Post-process methods such as TAA, SMAA and FXAA are cheap; MSAA can be very expensive in modern engines and is rarely offered anymore. When an upscaler is active, it usually takes over anti-aliasing entirely.

Post-processing – motion blur, depth of field, film grain, chromatic aberration, bloom – is mostly a matter of taste and usually cheap. Turn off what you dislike; do not expect big performance gains from it.

Presets: why “High” is often the sweet spot

Presets bundle all options into one choice. The jump from the highest preset to the one below often saves a lot of performance for a modest visual loss, because “Ultra” settings tend to push shadows, volumetrics and reflections far beyond what is visible during play. A good approach is to start from “High”, set textures as high as your VRAM allows, and then decide on ray tracing separately.

After a patch, check presets again: developers sometimes change what a preset contains, and the same name can cost more or less than before. The Patch Decoder flags such lines with a tilde (~).

Frequently asked questions

Which graphics setting gives the most FPS?

On a GPU-limited system, render resolution – best lowered through an upscaler mode – usually gives by far the most. On a CPU-limited system, view distance and crowd density help more.

Does lowering textures increase FPS?

Usually only when video memory is full. If the textures fit in VRAM, lowering them costs image quality for little or no gain.

Is Ultra worth it?

Often not. The step from High to Ultra typically costs much more performance than the visible improvement suggests. Test both in one fixed scene and decide.

Why do my settings perform differently after an update?

Updates can change what a preset contains, add effects or rebuild the shader cache. Re-test before changing anything, using the same scene as before.
