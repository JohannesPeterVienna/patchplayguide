Setting-name map: the same setting under different names

Published by AdStern, Vienna

Reviewed November 18, 20264 min readHow we check pages

A graphics menu is full of brand names. NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Sony, Microsoft, Nintendo and Valve each name the same ideas differently – and sometimes use the same word for different things. This map lists the names you meet in game menus, driver panels and console settings, says in one sentence what each one is, and links the official document it was taken from. Search for the word on your screen.

How the map was compiled

Every entry comes from a document published by the company that makes the product: developer guides in the vendors’ public repositories, platform support pages and official announcements. Nothing in the map is our own measurement or opinion about quality. Where a vendor publishes a number – a scale factor, a minimum frame rate, a requirement – the entry repeats that number and names the source in square brackets; the numbered list is under Sources, with the date the sources were read.

Two kinds of sources were used, and the list says which is which. Developer documents from NVIDIA, AMD and Intel were read in full. For support pages of Sony, Microsoft, Nintendo, Valve and for two AMD and NVIDIA product pages, the relevant passage was taken from a search-engine extract of the official page; these entries are worded more cautiously. Names we could not tie to an official source are left out instead of guessed – that is why, for example, NVIDIA’s G-SYNC display tiers are missing.

One job, several names

Most of the names fall into seven jobs. If you know the job, the brand name stops being a puzzle:

A dash means: no name from this vendor in the sources used for this map – not necessarily that nothing exists.

The split between “in the game” and “in the driver” matters in practice. A feature built into the game receives motion vectors, depth and the position of the interface from the game; a driver-level feature sees only finished images. AMD describes the difference for its two latency features: the driver version aligns CPU and GPU work from outside, while Anti-Lag 2 places its wait directly before the game reads your input.

Factors as documented by NVIDIA, AMD and Intel for developers. The percentage is the internal resolution per axis; the share of pixels is its square.

The preset names are the most common trap. “Quality” means 66.7% of the output resolution per axis with DLSS and FSR but 58.8% with XeSS 1.3 and newer. “Balanced” means 58% with DLSS, 58.8% with FSR and 50% with XeSS. So a like-for-like comparison of two upscalers at “the same” preset compares different pixel counts. The Upscaler Resolution Calculator turns the factors into the exact internal resolution for your display.

A second trap is the word “VRR” on Windows. Variable refresh rate on the Graphics settings page is the game feature; Dynamic refresh rate on the Advanced display page lets Windows change the desktop refresh rate on its own. The names differ by one word and do different things.

How to use it after a patch

Patch notes mention these names constantly: “updated to FSR 4”, “added XeSS-FG”, “Reflex support”. Search the name here to see which job it does, then decide what to re-test. An upscaler update can change image quality at the same preset; a new frame-generation option needs a look at the rendered frame rate first (the Frame Time Calculator shows shown versus rendered frames); a new latency option is worth switching on and comparing. The Patch Decoder sorts such lines, and how to read patch notes explains the wording.

Menus and requirements change with driver and system updates. Each entry reflects the linked document on the date given. If a name has changed on your system, please tell us with the source – corrections are logged in the change log below.

Frequently asked questions

Is DLSS Balanced the same as XeSS Balanced?

No. NVIDIA documents 1.724× per axis for DLSS Balanced (58% of the output resolution), Intel documents 2.0× for XeSS Balanced since version 1.3 (50%), which equals DLSS Performance.

What is the difference between Anti-Lag and Anti-Lag 2?

Radeon Anti-Lag works purely in the driver. Anti-Lag 2 is integrated by the game’s developer and places its delay just before the game samples your input, which AMD describes as a significantly greater latency reduction.

What is the difference between DLSS Frame Generation and Smooth Motion?

DLSS Frame Generation is built into a game by its developer. Smooth Motion is a driver feature that adds one interpolated frame between two rendered frames in games without DLSS Frame Generation and is switched on per game in the NVIDIA app.

Are FreeSync, G-SYNC and VRR the same thing?

They are names for variable refresh rate: the display waits for the next frame instead of refreshing at fixed intervals. AMD’s documentation lists AMD FreeSync, NVIDIA G-SYNC and VESA AdaptiveSync as VRR technologies; consoles call the HDMI version simply VRR.
